## 1. What the business is

### One sentence

We get businesses named and cited inside AI-generated answers, and we measure it.

### The longer version

When someone asks ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, or Google's AI Overviews
for a recommendation, they do not get a list of ten blue links. They get a
written answer that names three or four businesses. Everyone else is invisible —
not ranked low, **absent**.

Most businesses do not know this is happening to them. There is no dashboard
that tells them. There is no Search Console for AI answers.

We do three things:

1. **Measure** where a business stands across the major AI engines, against
   named competitors, using a fixed and repeatable prompt set.
2. **Fix** what we find — on-site work so answers can be extracted from their
   content, off-site work so the engines trust and cite them.
3. **Report** movement monthly, per engine, with the same prompt set, so
   progress is provable rather than asserted.

### Why we win business

Not because we are cheaper. Because of two things:

**Proof at first contact.** We do not pitch. We show the prospect a screenshot
of an AI engine naming their competitors and not them. That is evidence, not
marketing. It converts because it is specific and it stings.

**Measurement as the product.** Most vendors in this space sell vibes —
"AI-optimised content" with nothing behind it. We run a fixed prompt set,
repeatedly, under controlled conditions, and report numbers. Clients who care
about measurement are the clients who stay.

### What we are not

- Not an SEO agency. Related discipline, different mechanics, different metrics.
- Not a content mill.
- Not a tool vendor. We are a service. Tools may support us; they are not the
  product.
- Not a guarantee business. See Section 6.

## 2. Positioning and language

### The two disciplines we deliver

Clients do not need to know these terms. You do, because they map to different
work, different timelines, and different reporting.

**AEO — Answer Engine Optimization.** Making the business's content the thing an
engine **extracts** as a direct answer. Surfaces: featured snippets, People Also
Ask, voice assistants, Google AI Overviews. Mostly **on-site** work. Faster to
move.

**GEO — Generative Engine Optimization.** Making the business a **cited source**
when an LLM synthesises an answer. Surfaces: ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity,
Gemini. Heavily **off-site** work — third-party mentions, reviews, original data,
entity consistency. Slower, more defensible.

| | AEO | GEO |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Extraction — engine lifts your text | Synthesis — engine blends sources, you are one |
| Where the work is | Mostly on-site | Mostly off-site |
| Winner | Usually one page wins | Multiple sources blend |
| Metric | Did we get extracted — binary, per query | Citation share across many prompts — probabilistic |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |

**Simplest framing:** AEO wants you to *be* the answer. GEO wants you to be in
the set of sources the answer was *built from*.

### What to say to clients

Sell one thing: **AI Search Visibility.** Do not lead with acronyms.

The client's actual question is always the same: *"Why doesn't my business come
up when someone asks ChatGPT?"* Answer that.

Explain the AEO/GEO distinction only when the buyer is technical. Then it becomes
a credibility asset, because most vendors cannot articulate the difference.

### Language rules

**Use:** visible, cited, named, measured, baseline, prompt set, citation share,
mention rate.

**Avoid:** guaranteed, ranked #1, dominate, unlock, revolutionary, game-changing,
10x, hack, secret. Also avoid "AI-powered" as a description of our service — we
are a service applied to AI systems, not an AI product.

## 4. The ideal client

This is the most important operational section. A wrong client costs three months
and ends in a refund request.

### The three-part test

A prospect must pass all three.

**1. A new customer is worth $2,000 or more to them.**
If one new customer is worth $5,000, a $1,500 audit pays for itself with a single
conversion. If a customer is worth $50, you will never justify the fee. This
single filter matters more than every other consideration combined.

**2. They already spend money on marketing.**
You are redirecting a budget, not creating one. Businesses with no marketing
spend have no mental category for this purchase.

**3. Their competitor is cited and they are not.**
This is the emotional trigger. No gap, no urgency, no sale. If a prospect is
already well cited, they are not a prospect — they are a reference case.

### Target verticals

Ranked by fit. Pick **one** and stay in it until you have three case studies.

| Vertical | Why it fits |
|---|---|
| Cosmetic and implant dentistry | Very high per-patient value, competitive local markets, owner-operated |
| Med spas / aesthetics | High ticket, high growth, marketing-literate buyers |
| Elective and private healthcare | High value, trust-driven purchase, AI-researched by patients |
| Personal injury / immigration law | Extremely high case value, already heavy marketing spenders |
| B2B SaaS | Understands the problem instantly, sophisticated on measurement |
| Wealth / insurance advisory | High lifetime value, credibility-led purchase |
| High-value home services (solar, roofing) | Large ticket sizes, local competition |

**Avoid:** restaurants, retail, low-ticket local services, anything with a
sub-$500 customer value, and anything with no digital dependency.

### One vertical or several — the strategy

Two decisions get conflated here. Separate them.

| Decision | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Positioning** — what the site, LinkedIn and pitch say | **One vertical. Always.** |
| **Prospecting** — who you actually email | One primary + one adjacent test, **first 60 days only** |

Showing multiple verticals in your positioning makes you a generalist. Generalists
discount; specialists command a premium.

**Why one vertical compounds:**

- **Prompt set reuse.** A new vertical costs 2–3 hours of prompt-set work. A new
  client in an existing vertical costs 30–45 minutes. Build once, reuse forever.
- **Off-site knowledge does not transfer.** The directories, review platforms and
  industry publications that engines pull from are completely different for dental
  versus legal. After five clients in one vertical you know exactly which six
  sources matter. That knowledge is your margin.
- **Case studies only convert inside a vertical.** "Three dental clinics" converts
  a dentist. "One dentist, one lawyer, one SaaS" converts nobody.
- **Referrals only travel inside a vertical.** Dentists know dentists. This is how
  outbound eventually stops.
- **Pricing power comes from specificity.** "AI search visibility for cosmetic
  dentistry" prices better than "AI visibility for businesses".

**Why not to bet 100% on one either:** you have no data yet. Committing entirely to
one vertical and discovering its owners do not reply costs four weeks.

**The hedge:** 40 touches in the primary vertical, 15–20 in an adjacent backup.
Compare reply rates. At 60 days, kill the loser and go all in on the winner. This
is cheap because you adapt one prompt set rather than building two.

**The backup must be adjacent.** Adjacency is what keeps the transfer cost low.

- **Good pair:** cosmetic/implant dentistry ↔ med spa / aesthetics. Same buyer
  psychology (elective, high ticket, local, owner-operated), overlapping
  directories, similar prompt shapes. Roughly 60–70% of the prompt set carries over.
- **Bad pair:** cosmetic dentistry ↔ B2B SaaS. Different buyer, sales cycle,
  prompts, off-site levers and pricing. That is two businesses, not one.

### Expand geography, not vertical

This is the rule most people get backwards.

When the dental clinics in Dubai are exhausted, move to Abu Dhabi, then Riyadh,
then London, then Toronto. Same prompt set, same off-site playbook, same case
studies, same accumulated pattern recognition. Only the city changes.

Changing vertical resets you to zero. Changing geography keeps everything.

**Add a second vertical only when all three are true:**

1. Three case studies exist in the current vertical
2. Referrals are arriving unprompted
3. Three or more cities have been worked through

Realistically that is 8–12 months out.

### Current selection

**Primary: cosmetic and implant dentistry, Dubai.** Highest per-patient value,
~1.5 hour timezone gap, owner-operated single decision makers, English-language
business environment, and a purchase buyers actively research through AI.

**Backup test: med spas / aesthetics, Dubai.** Adjacent, so the prompt set and
off-site knowledge transfer.

**Review date: 60 days from first outreach.** Kill the weaker performer, then scale
by city.

### Geography

Pricing forces Western and Gulf markets. An Indian SMB will not pay ₹1.7L/month
for a service it does not yet understand.

| Market | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **UAE / GCC** | **Best first target.** High-ticket clinics and firms, Western-level rates, English-language business environment, and a timezone gap of only ~1.5 hours from India — meaning same-day conversation instead of next-day. |
| US | Largest market, most budget. Timezone gap of 9–12 hours makes iteration slow. |
| UK / EU | Good rates, manageable timezone, EAA has raised general digital-compliance awareness. |
| Canada / Australia | Viable, smaller volume. |
| **India (B2B SaaS selling globally)** | Viable secondary. Their buyers are Western, so the problem is real. Lower price point, but far easier trust and same timezone. Realistic source of a first client. |

### Stage 1 qualification — desk research, 5 minutes per prospect

Run this **before** any contact. It saves more time than anything else in this
handbook.

| # | Check | How to verify | Fail means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer value $2,000+ | Services and prices on their site | Skip — fee never justifiable |
| 2 | Already spending | Google Ads running, agency credit in footer, recent blog activity, marketing roles posted on LinkedIn | Skip — no budget category |
| 3 | Visibility gap exists | Run 3–5 prompts from the vertical prompt set | Skip — no pain, no sale |
| 4 | Site is modifiable | Identifiable CMS, or a developer they can reach | Skip — cannot deliver AEO |
| 5 | Decision maker reachable | Owner-operated, or small enough that the owner decides | Skip — procurement will kill a $1,500 deal |
| 6 | Not already served | No in-house SEO team, no large incumbent agency | Redirect — approach **the agency** for white-label work instead |

**Scoring: 4 or more passes → add to outreach list. 3 or fewer → skip.**

Check 6 is frequently misplayed. Where an agency already holds the account, the
client is not your prospect — the agency is. They have the client relationship
and no AI-visibility capability. That is a partnership, not a competition.

### Stage 2 qualification — on the call

Six questions. Ask them early; disqualify fast.

1. **"What is a new client worth to you over their lifetime?"**
   Does not know, or the number is small → out.
2. **"What are you spending on marketing now, and with whom?"**
   Confirms budget exists and reveals the incumbent.
3. **"Who can make changes to your website, and how quickly?"**
   Delivery feasibility. A locked platform is a hard blocker.
4. **"If this works, who signs off on continuing?"**
   Decision authority.
5. **"What would you need to see in 90 days to keep going?"**
   Sets expectations and exposes impossible demands early.
6. **"Have you tried anything like this before?"**
   Previously burned is harder to sell but often more sophisticated.

### Red flags — walk away

- Asks for a **guarantee**, or "pay on results". The single biggest flag. LLM
  outputs are non-deterministic; a guarantee would be a lie.
- Wants a promised ranking or position.
- Expects complete results within weeks.
- Negotiates the audit price down hard on the first call.
- Needs three people to approve a $1,500 decision.
- Site is on a locked platform they cannot modify.
- Business is entirely offline walk-in with no digital dependency.

### Green flags — prioritise

- Reacts **emotionally** to the competitor screenshot. Strongest buying signal
  there is.
- Already aware AI search matters, and worried about it.
- Asks **"how will you measure this?"** — sophisticated buyer, will value the
  differentiator, and will retain.
- Owner-operated, single decision maker.
- High-ticket service.

### The discipline

You are one person. Your scarcest resource is time, not leads. Disqualifying is
not lost revenue — it is protected capacity.

## 6. Expectations versus reality

The most important section for avoiding refunds. Read before every sales call.

### The core mechanic clients do not know

AI search runs on **two separate memory systems**, and only one of them is
reachable.

**1. The retrieval layer — reachable.** Perplexity runs a live web search on
essentially every query. ChatGPT Search and Google AI Overviews also pull live
content. A new authoritative source can begin shaping answers within **hours to
weeks**. [FiveBlocks, Forrester]

**2. The training layer — not reachable.** A model's baseline knowledge only
changes when the model is retrained or fine-tuned, a cycle measured in **months**.
Claude anchors primarily to its training corpus, using Brave Search only when
retrieval is triggered. [Broworks]

**Everything we sell targets the retrieval layer.** We never promise anything
about the training layer. It is outside our control and outside anyone's.

### Engines are not interchangeable

When the same prompt is run across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google AI
Overviews, the overlap in cited domains is roughly **11%**. Four platforms, four
almost entirely separate source lists. [Topify]

Consequences:

- "We'll make you visible in AI" is a meaningless promise. Work and reporting are
  **per engine**.
- Six engines means roughly six times the measurement labour. Price accordingly.
- Wins arrive on different engines at different times.

One more useful data point: businesses with a detailed Wikipedia entry get their
first ChatGPT citation in an average of **28 days**, versus **52 days** without
one. Wikipedia alone contributes about **7.8%** of ChatGPT's citations and makes
up roughly **48%** of its top-10 cited sources. [Topify]

### What the client assumes versus what is true

| Client assumes | Reality |
|---|---|
| "You can get me to #1 in ChatGPT" | There is no #1. Answers are generated and non-deterministic. The same prompt can return different answers on consecutive runs. |
| "This is just SEO" | Related but distinct. Google rankings and AI citations do not map one-to-one. |
| "I'll see results in two weeks" | Crawler access changes in days. First citations typically 4–12 weeks. Consistent visibility 3–6 months. |
| "You control what the AI says" | We influence which sources it can find and trust. We do not control output. |
| "One engine's result applies everywhere" | ~11% citation overlap between engines. |
| "If it doesn't work I get my money back" | The audit is a diagnostic deliverable. It is complete when delivered. No results-based refund. |

### The honest timeline

Present this table on sales calls. Do not soften it.

| Period | What happens | Verified by |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–7 | Crawler access and indexable answers corrected. Fixable in days. | LeanLabs |
| Weeks 1–2 | First AI mentions may appear. Volatile. Signal, not a win. | Higoodie |
| Weeks 4–8 | Early signals, impressions rising. First mentions commonly 30–60 days. | WebFX, AirOps |
| Weeks 4–12 | Citations begin on specific, low-competition prompts. | LeanLabs |
| Months 3–6 | Repeat citations, consistent visibility across engines. | WebFX, Upgrowth |

**The sentence to say:** *"Month one is plumbing. Months two and three are when
citations start. Months three to six are when visibility becomes consistent."*

Promise anything faster and you will be issuing refunds.

### The churn problem, and the answer

Results take 3–6 months. Retainers churn at month 2 or 3 if the client sees
nothing. That is the central operational risk in this business.

The answer is not to overpromise. It is to **report leading indicators in weeks
1–4** — things that move before citations do and are independently verifiable:

1. **Crawler hits from server logs.** The strongest week-one proof. A 30-day site
   log study found GPTBot to be the most aggressive crawler at roughly **4,200
   hits per site per day**, ClaudeBot at ~1,800, PerplexityBot at ~980, and all
   four major bots respecting robots.txt. [Digital Applied] So when you unblock
   crawler access, the effect appears in logs **the next day**. "These bots
   weren't reaching your site at all; now they're here daily" is a real,
   verifiable win in week one.
2. **Schema validation** — before and after, via Google's Rich Results Test.
3. **Prompt-set coverage** — a mention without a citation is still movement.
   Report it.
4. **Featured snippets and PAA** — these move faster than LLM citations. AEO's
   speed advantage.
5. **Third-party placements secured** — reportable the moment they go live, before
   any engine cites them.

This discipline is what keeps a client to month three.

### What we do promise

## 7. How AI search actually works — technical foundation

Everything we do maps to something in this section. Read it before delivering.

### The five surfaces

| Engine | Retrieval behaviour | Implication for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Perplexity** | Live web search on essentially every query, continuously refreshed index, shows sources by design | **Fastest to move. Win here first.** |
| **ChatGPT Search** | Blends training knowledge with a web search layer. Browsing on → days. Browsing off → months. | Medium speed. Wikipedia presence matters here more than elsewhere. |
| **Google AI Overviews** | Tied to Google's index | Follows normal indexing cadence — weeks |
| **Gemini** | Google index-backed | Similar to AI Overviews |
| **Claude** | Anchors primarily to training corpus; Brave Search when retrieval triggers | **Slowest.** Set expectations accordingly. |

Sources: Broworks, FiveBlocks, DataStudios, Forrester.

**Engine sequencing strategy.** Do not attack all engines at once. Win Perplexity
first — it is the fastest and gives you a month-one reportable result. Then AI
Overviews and ChatGPT. Broad consistency by month three.

### What makes an engine cite a source

Three requirements, in order. Each is a failure point.

**1. Access.** The engine's crawler must be able to fetch the page.
Blockers: `robots.txt` disallow, JavaScript-only content, cookie walls,
interstitials, accidental `noindex`, login gates.
If a crawler cannot read it, nothing else matters.

**2. Extractability.** The content must be structured so an answer can be lifted
from it cleanly.
Requirements: answer-first paragraphs, question-shaped headings, real semantic
HTML, genuine `<table>` markup with `<th scope>`, self-contained statements with
no "as mentioned above", valid structured data.

**3. Trust.** The engine must consider the source credible enough to name.
Signals: independent third-party mentions, editorial coverage, reviews on trusted
platforms, presence in knowledge bases, consistent entity information, original
data worth citing, freshness.

Point 3 is where GEO lives and it is mostly **off-site**. This is why GEO is
slower and why it is more defensible once achieved.

### The AI crawlers

Configure access for all of these:

```
GPTBot              OpenAI, training
OAI-SearchBot       OpenAI, search index
ChatGPT-User        OpenAI, user-triggered fetch
ClaudeBot           Anthropic
anthropic-ai        Anthropic
PerplexityBot       Perplexity
Google-Extended     Google, Gemini/AI training control
Applebot-Extended   Apple
CCBot               Common Crawl (feeds many models)
Bingbot             Bing, feeds ChatGPT search
```

Observed crawl volumes from a 30-day site log study: GPTBot ~4,200 hits/site/day,
ClaudeBot ~1,800, PerplexityBot ~980. All four major bots respected robots.txt.
[Digital Applied]

**Operational consequence:** GPTBot crawls daily, so an access fix shows up in
logs within 24–48 hours. That is your first reportable win on every engagement.

### `llms.txt`

A plain-text file at the site root summarising who the business is, what it
offers, and where the canonical pages are. Adoption is not universal across
engines and its impact is not independently proven at the level schema is. Treat
it as low-cost, low-risk, not as a primary lever. Do not oversell it to clients.

### Structured data that matters

| Schema type | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `Organization` / `LocalBusiness` | Site-wide | Core entity definition |
| Industry-specific (`Dentist`, `MedicalClinic`, `LegalService`) | Site-wide | Category precision |
| `Service` | Each service page | What is offered |
| `FAQPage` | FAQ and any page with Q&A | Direct extraction target |
| `Article` + author + dates | Content pages | Authority and freshness |
| `DefinedTerm` / `DefinedTermSet` | Glossary pages | Definitional content is disproportionately cited |
| `BreadcrumbList` | Nested pages | Structure comprehension |
| `HowTo` | Process pages | Step extraction |
| `Review` / `AggregateRating` | Where genuine | Trust signal — only if real |

Validate everything with Google's Rich Results Test. Never mark up content that
is not visible on the page.

### Content structure for extraction

The single highest-leverage on-site change:

**Answer-first.** After every H2, the very next thing is a direct,
self-contained, 40–60 word answer to that heading. Detail follows after.

Also required:

- Headings shaped as real buyer questions, taken from the prompt set
- Comparison tables as real tables
- Every claim self-contained — an engine may lift one paragraph with no context
- Statistics with visible source attribution
- `dateModified` surfaced visibly; stale content drops out of synthesis
- Glossary and definitional pages — LLMs cite clean definitions heavily

### Off-site levers, ranked by leverage

1. **Third-party "best X for Y" listicles.** The highest-leverage lever, because
   this is precisely what engines pull from for recommendation queries.
2. **Original data or statistics.** One small original dataset earns more
   citations than fifty blog posts. Nothing is more citable than a number nobody
   else has.
3. **Review platforms and credible industry directories.**
4. **Editorial mentions in trusted publications.**
5. **Entity consistency** — identical name, category, and description everywhere.
6. **Wikipedia / Wikidata** — measurably significant (28 vs 52 days to first
   ChatGPT citation) **but only where the business genuinely meets notability
   requirements.** If it does not, the entry will be removed and the relationship
   damaged. Never sell this as a shortcut.

## 8. The prompt set — the core instrument

Everything measurable in this business comes from here. Build it first, build it
properly, and then never change it mid-engagement.

### Why it must be fixed

Baseline comparison is the entire product. If the prompt set changes, month three
cannot be compared to month zero, and there is no proof of anything. Version it.
Additions go into a new version, documented, and reported separately.

### Composition — 40 to 50 prompts

Five categories. Proportions are a starting point, adjust per vertical.

| Category | Share | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Category discovery** | ~30% | "best [service] in [city]" |
| **Comparison** | ~20% | "[option A] vs [option B] cost and results" |
| **Problem-led** | ~25% | "how do I fix [problem the service solves]" |
| **Brand-direct** | ~15% | "is [brand] any good", "[brand] reviews" |
| **Local / qualified** | ~10% | "[service] near [landmark]", "affordable [service] [city]" |

Build these from how buyers actually speak, not from keyword tools. Keyword tools
give you search syntax; AI prompts are conversational sentences.

### Run methodology — conditions must be identical every time

Non-negotiable, or the data is worthless:

1. **Three runs per prompt per engine.** LLM outputs are non-deterministic. One
   run tells you nothing.
2. **Clean session.** Logged out, no history, no personalisation. A fresh browser
   profile or incognito.
3. **Consistent geography.** Same location every run. Note it. Use a consistent
   VPN exit if targeting a market you are not in.
4. **Same day of week, similar time of day.**
5. **Record verbatim.** Capture the actual answer text and the cited URLs, not a
   summary.
6. **Screenshot every result where the client is absent and a competitor is
   present.** These are your sales assets and your report evidence.

### What to record per run

| Field | Values |
|---|---|
| Prompt ID | P01–P50 |
| Prompt text | verbatim |
| Engine | ChatGPT / Perplexity / Gemini / AI Overviews / Claude |
| Run number | 1, 2, 3 |
| Date, time, location | — |
| Client mentioned | yes / no |
| Client cited with link | yes / no |
| Position in answer | 1st / 2nd / 3rd / later / absent |
| Competitors named | list, in order |
| Cited domains | full list of URLs |
| Screenshot reference | filename |

### The two headline metrics

**Mention rate** = (runs where client is named) ÷ (total runs) × 100
Per engine. Reported as a percentage.

**Citation share** = (runs where client is cited with a link) ÷ (total runs) × 100
Per engine. Stricter and more valuable than mention rate.

Also report **competitive position**: for each competitor, their mention rate
alongside the client's. This is what makes the report land emotionally.

### Worked example

40 prompts × 3 runs × 5 engines = **600 data points** per full cycle.

If the client is named in 24 of 120 ChatGPT runs:
mention rate on ChatGPT = 24 ÷ 120 = **20%**.

If cited with a link in 9 of those:
citation share on ChatGPT = 9 ÷ 120 = **7.5%**.

Report each engine separately. Never average across engines — the ~11% overlap
makes a blended number meaningless.

### Time cost — know this before pricing

| Task | Estimated time |
|---|---|
| Build prompt set for a new vertical | 2–3 h (reusable afterwards) |
| Adapt existing set to a new client | 30–45 min |
| Run 600 data points | 4–6 h |
| Record and clean data | 1–2 h |
| Analysis and diagnosis | 3–4 h |
| Write the report | 3–4 h |
| **Total first audit** | **13–19 h** |
| **Monthly re-run + report** | **6–9 h** |

*These are estimates from task decomposition, not measured averages. Track your
actual hours on the first three clients and replace them.*

At $1,500 for 13–19 hours, the effective rate is roughly $80–115/hour. That is
sound. Do not discount below the founding-client rate.

### Tooling

**Start entirely manual.** Browser plus a spreadsheet. Cost: zero.

Do this for the first three clients even though it is tedious. Reasons: you learn
what the data actually looks like, you can explain every number to a client, and
you will be able to tell when a paid tool is wrong.

Consider a dedicated AI-visibility tracking tool only at 4+ clients, when the
manual run becomes the bottleneck. Buying a tool before having clients is the most
common way to lose money in this business.

Fastest ROI in the entire engagement. These changes propagate in days.

## 10. Reporting

The report is the product the client actually holds. It justifies the retainer.

### Audit report structure

1. **Executive summary** — one page. Where they stand, the three biggest gaps,
   what to do first. Written for someone who reads nothing else.
2. **Baseline scorecard** — mention rate and citation share, per engine, in a
   table. Numbers, not adjectives.
3. **Competitive position** — the client versus up to three named competitors, per
   engine. The most persuasive page in the document.
4. **Evidence** — the screenshots. Client absent, competitor present, prompt shown.
5. **Diagnosis** — organised by the three requirements from Section 7: access,
   extractability, trust. Each finding states what is wrong, why it matters, and
   what fixes it.
6. **Prioritised roadmap** — ranked by expected movement against effort. Marked
   as on-site or off-site, with an expected timeframe drawn from Section 6.
7. **Methodology appendix** — the prompt set, run conditions, dates, definitions
   of every metric. This is what separates a real audit from a generated one.

### Monthly report structure

Shorter. Four pages maximum.

1. **Movement summary** — mention rate and citation share this cycle versus
   baseline versus last cycle, per engine.
2. **What moved and why** — tie changes to specific work completed.
3. **Leading indicators** — crawler activity, placements secured, schema status,
   snippets gained. Especially important in months 1–3 when citations are still
   thin.
4. **Next cycle's priorities** — the fix list, ranked.

### Reporting rules

- **Report bad months honestly.** Non-deterministic systems fluctuate. A dip
  explained holds a client; a dip hidden loses them permanently when discovered.
- **Never average across engines.** Report each separately.
- **Always show the comparison** — versus baseline and versus named competitors.
  Absolute numbers alone mean nothing to a client.
- **Attribute honestly.** If a change cannot be tied to your work, say so.
- Same format every month. Consistency is itself a credibility signal.

## 11. Client acquisition

### Channel priority

Ranked for a solo operator with no case studies. Spend roughly 70% of sales time
on channel 1.

| # | Channel | Why | Speed to first client |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Direct outbound with a personalised audit finding** | You arrive with evidence, not a pitch | 2–4 weeks |
| 2 | **Agency / web-developer partnerships** | They hold clients and lack this capability. Recurring, zero marketing. | 3–6 weeks, then compounding |
| 3 | **Niche communities where owners already gather** | Value first, then inbound DMs | 3–6 weeks |
| 4 | **LinkedIn content — anonymised audit findings** | Builds inbound over time | 6–12 weeks |
| 5 | **Freelance platforms — specific keywords only** | Buyers ready to pay, but you look like a commodity | Variable. Backup only. |

**Not now:** paid ads (no conversion data yet), cold calling without proof,
generic "AI agency" broadcasting, presence on every platform.

### Channel 1 — the core motion

The entire advantage is that you show up with proof of *their* specific gap.

**Step 1 — Build the list (30–45 min for 50 prospects).**
Google Maps, industry directories, or LinkedIn for the chosen vertical and city.
Capture: business name, website, owner name, email, LinkedIn URL.

**Step 2 — Qualify (5 min each, Section 4 Stage 1).**
Score all 50. Keep those scoring 4+. Expect roughly 20–30 to survive.

**Step 3 — Run the mini-audit (3–4 h for the surviving list).**
Five core prompts per prospect across ChatGPT, Perplexity and AI Overviews.
Record: is the prospect named? which competitors are? Screenshot every gap.

**Step 4 — Personalised outreach.**
One email or DM per prospect, each referencing their own gap and attaching their
own screenshot.

**Step 5 — Follow up.** Two follow-ups. Most replies come from these, not the
first message.

### Outreach templates

Adapt every one. Never send generic.

**First touch — email**

> Subject: [Competitor] comes up in ChatGPT, [Their business] doesn't
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I ran a few searches the way your patients now do — asking ChatGPT and
> Perplexity instead of Google.
>
> For "[actual prompt]", the answer named [Competitor A] and [Competitor B].
> [Their business] wasn't mentioned. Screenshot attached.
>
> These are people actively choosing a [service] provider, and they're choosing
> without seeing you. It's fixable — the reason is usually technical, not
> reputational.
>
> Worth 15 minutes to walk you through what I found and what causes it?
>
> [Name] · benamedfirst.com

Why this works: specific, evidenced, no adjectives, no pitch, one small ask.

**Follow-up 1 — 4 days later**

> Hi [Name], following up on the ChatGPT search I sent.
>
> One thing worth adding: I checked which sources those engines *are* pulling
> from for [service] in [city]. Three of them are directories you're either not
> listed on or listed inconsistently. That's a large part of the gap and it's a
> straightforward fix.
>
> Happy to send the list whether or not we ever work together — want it?

Gives value unconditionally. Frequently the reply-generating message.

**Follow-up 2 — 8 days after that, closing the loop**

> Hi [Name], I'll stop here so I'm not cluttering your inbox.
>
> If it's useful later, the summary is: for [prompt], AI engines currently name
> [Competitor A] and [Competitor B] and not you. My notes are attached — yours to
> use with anyone.
>
> If it becomes a priority, you know where I am.

Non-needy, leaves the door open, leaves value behind. A meaningful share of
replies come from this one.

**LinkedIn DM — shorter**

> Hi [Name] — I ran "[prompt]" through ChatGPT and Perplexity. Both named
> [Competitor] and not [Business]. Happy to send the screenshots and the reason —
> want them?

**Agency partnership email**

> Subject: The AI-visibility question your clients are starting to ask
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Your clients have probably started asking why they don't appear when someone
> asks ChatGPT for a recommendation. It's a different discipline from SEO —
> different signals, different measurement, no Search Console equivalent.
>
> I do this work white-label for agencies. You keep the relationship and the
> margin; I deliver measurement and implementation under your brand.
>
> I've run a sample check on one of your clients — [Client] doesn't appear for
> [prompt] while two competitors do. Screenshot attached, no charge.
>
> Worth a conversation?

### Volume mathematics

Estimates from typical proof-led outbound response patterns. Track your own and
replace them.

| Stage | Rate | From 50 qualified prospects |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified after Stage 1 | ~50% of raw list | 25 |
| Reply to personalised, evidenced outreach | 15–25% | 4–6 |
| Reply → call booked | ~60% | 3–4 |
| Call → audit sold | 30–50% | 1–2 |

**Roughly 40–50 personalised touches produces 1–2 audits.**

Complete the full set before judging the channel. Sending five emails and
concluding "it doesn't work" is the most common failure mode.

### The specificity multiplier

The largest lever is not the channel — it is specificity.

| Message | Reply rate |
|---|---|
| "I do AI SEO for businesses" | ~1% |
| "Your clinic is invisible in ChatGPT — here's the screenshot" | 15–20% |

Same channel, roughly 15x difference. Every channel follows this rule: never go
generic, always lead with their own gap.

### Email deliverability — do not skip

Get this wrong and outreach fails silently. No bounce, no error, just no replies.

- Never send cold outreach from personal Gmail or Outlook
- Use a domain mailbox: `you@benamedfirst.com` (Google Workspace or Zoho)
- Configure **SPF, DKIM and DMARC** DNS records before the first send
- Warm the domain: 10–20 sends/day in week one, increase gradually. A new domain
  blasting 100 emails on day one gets burned
- Test with a deliverability checker before any real send
- Plain text, one link maximum, no tracking pixels, no images in the first email

### Tracking

From day one, record: touches sent, replies, calls booked, proposals sent, deals
closed. Per channel, per vertical.

Once you know that 45 touches produces one client, "no work coming in" stops being
panic and becomes arithmetic: send 45 more.

## 12. Sales and closing

### Call structure — 30 minutes

| Minutes | Purpose | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Frame | "I'll show you what I found, then you decide if it's worth pursuing." |
| 3–10 | **Show the evidence** | Share screen. Run the prompt live if you can. Let them see competitors named and themselves absent. Then stop talking. |
| 10–18 | Qualify | The six questions from Section 4 Stage 2 |
| 18–24 | Explain and scope | What causes it, what the audit produces, honest timeline from Section 6 |
| 24–28 | Price and terms | The number, what is included, what is not |
| 28–30 | Close | One clear next step |

**The silence at minute 10 is the most important part of the call.** Show the
evidence and stop. Let them react. Their reaction tells you whether this closes.

### Running the demo live

If possible, run a prompt live on the call rather than showing a screenshot. It is
dramatically more persuasive because it cannot be doubted.

Prepare: clean browser profile, logged out, correct location, and two or three
prompts you have already verified show the gap. Have screenshots as backup in case
of a bad connection or an unexpectedly different answer.

### Objection handling

**"How do I know this works?"**
> You don't yet, and I won't promise it. What I can promise is measurement. We
> establish exactly where you stand today, then report the same numbers monthly.
> If nothing moves, you'll see that clearly. Most vendors in this space can't tell
> you either way — that's the actual problem.

**"Can you guarantee I'll show up in ChatGPT?"**
> No, and anyone who does is lying to you. These are non-deterministic systems —
> the same question can return different answers on consecutive runs. What I
> control is whether the engines can access your content, whether it's structured
> to be extracted, and whether credible sources reference you. Those are the
> inputs. I report the outputs honestly.

**"Why not just do SEO?"**
> You should still do SEO. But Google rankings and AI citations don't map
> one-to-one — I can show you queries where you rank on page one and still don't
> appear in the AI answer. Different mechanism, different work.

**"This is expensive."**
> What's a new [patient/client] worth to you? [Listen.] So the audit costs less
> than [fraction] of one. And you keep the roadmap whether or not you continue
> with me.

**"How long until results?"**
> Crawler access changes within days and I can show you that in your server logs
> in week one. First citations typically 4 to 12 weeks on lower-competition
> queries. Consistent visibility across engines, 3 to 6 months. Anyone promising
> faster is selling you something.

**"Let me think about it."**
> Of course. What specifically would you want to be more confident about?
> [Then address only that.]

**"Send me a proposal."**
> Happy to. Before I do — is there anything in what I've shown you that doesn't
> land yet? I'd rather resolve it now than in a document.

**"Can we do results-based pricing?"**
> No. With non-deterministic systems, outcomes aren't fully within anyone's
> control, and results-based pricing would push me toward whatever moves a number
> fastest rather than what's actually right for your business. I charge for the
> work and I report the results honestly.

### The proposal

One page. Send within 24 hours. Long proposals lose deals.

Contents: what you found (2–3 lines), scope, deliverables, timeline, price,
payment terms, what is explicitly excluded, start date, one clear acceptance
instruction.

### Terms to hold

- **Audit: 100% upfront.** Standard for fixed-scope diagnostics.
- **Implementation: 50/50.**
- **Retainer: monthly in advance, 3-month minimum.**
- No results-based pricing. No guarantees. No free pilots.
- One round of report revisions included; further rounds are billable.
- Scope changes are quoted separately in writing.

### Payment mechanics — set up before the first close

Nothing kills momentum like a client saying yes and then waiting three days for
your bank details.

- Wise Business or Payoneer for international transfers; Stripe for card payments
  where available
- An invoice template ready: your details, client details, scope line, amount,
  currency, due date, payment instructions
- **Consult a CA about export-of-services rules and GST/LUT for foreign clients
  before your first invoice.** This is a genuine compliance area and getting it
  wrong is expensive to unwind. This handbook does not give tax advice.

### Leading indicators to watch weekly

1. Touches sent
2. Reply rate
3. Calls booked
4. Audit-to-implementation conversion
5. Retainer count and churn
6. Hours per client (against Section 9 estimates)

Item 6 is the one people ignore. If a client consumes double the estimate, either
the scope grew or the price is wrong. Margin erosion from scope creep is the
documented reason most agencies in this space fail — not lack of demand.

## 15. Team, tools and delegation

### Do it all yourself for the first three clients

Not for cost reasons. For three specific ones:

1. You learn the **actual** time each task takes, which is the only sound basis
   for pricing.
2. You learn what can safely be delegated and what cannot.
3. You stay cash-flow positive with no fixed commitments.

Hiring before you have measured your own delivery is the fastest way to lose
money here.

### Work composition

Roughly, across a full engagement:

| Type | Share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Judgement and manual work | ~60% | Measurement, verification, client calls, strategy. This is what you are paid for. AI cannot do it. |
| AI-accelerated | ~30% | Content drafts, schema code, report summaries. You review everything. |
| Delegable grunt work | ~10% | Prompt running, data entry, listing cleanup |

### When to delegate, and what

Only after 4–5 clients, and task by task — not by hiring a team.

**Delegate first:** prompt running and data entry. A VA can execute a documented
run methodology and fill the scorecard. Biggest hour saving available.

**Delegate later:** content drafting (per-article freelancer), off-site outreach,
directory cleanup.

**Never delegate:** measurement methodology, technical and schema work, client
communication, final quality review. These are the moat.

Structure: a VA plus per-task freelancers. No fixed salaries. Variable cost only
when work exists.

### Tool stack

**From day one:**

| Tool | Purpose | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Browser + spreadsheet | Measurement | Free |
| Claude / ChatGPT paid | Content and code assistance | $20/mo |
| Google Rich Results Test | Schema validation | Free |
| Screaming Frog (free tier) | Technical crawl, up to 500 URLs | Free |
| Deliverability checker | Email testing | Free |
| VPN | Consistent geography for runs | $5–12/mo |
| Calendar booking tool | Call scheduling | Free tier |

**Only at 4+ clients:** a dedicated AI-visibility tracking platform. Run manually
first so you can tell when a tool is wrong, and so you can explain every number
to a client.

## 17. Integrity rules — binding

These are not guidelines. Breaking any one of them creates legal exposure,
destroys client trust, or both. They apply to everything: the website, proposals,
reports, outreach, social content, and anything an AI assistant produces from this
handbook.

### Never fabricate

- No invented client names, logos, testimonials, or case study numbers
- No invented statistics. If a figure is not in Section 18, do not state it as
  fact.
- No claimed awards, certifications, partnerships, or years of experience
- No implying a team exists. This is a solo practice — position it as a specialist
  practice, not a fake agency.
- No client names in examples without written permission. Anonymise as "a [vertical]
  business in [region]".
- No trademark symbols. The name is not registered.

On the website, build components for testimonials and case studies, populate them
with data explicitly flagged as placeholder, and do not publish until real ones
exist. A client can verify fabricated proof in minutes.

### Never guarantee

No guarantees of rankings, positions, citations, mention rates, timelines for
specific outcomes, or revenue impact. LLM outputs are non-deterministic; any
guarantee would be knowingly false.

Sell the process and honest measurement.

### Always attribute

Every statistic used publicly carries its source. Section 18 is the approved list.
When paraphrasing a source, do not distort its meaning.

### Verify AI output

AI drafts content; you verify every factual and service claim before it is
published or sent. In healthcare, legal, and financial verticals a hallucinated
claim can create genuine liability for the client and for you. You are the
reviewer of record.

### Respect the platforms

- Follow `robots.txt` conventions and platform terms
- No fake reviews, no incentivised reviews where prohibited, no review gating
- No Wikipedia entries for businesses that do not meet notability requirements
- No manipulation of engagement metrics

### Distinguish estimate from fact

This handbook contains both. Estimates — hour figures, funnel rates, revenue ramp —
are labelled. Never present an estimate to a client as a measured result.

### Handle client data carefully

Server logs, analytics access, and CMS credentials are sensitive. Store credentials
in a password manager, never in plain text or in chat. Never echo credentials into
a document or an AI prompt.

## 19. Glossary

**AEO — Answer Engine Optimization.** Structuring content so an engine extracts it
as a direct answer. Surfaces: featured snippets, People Also Ask, voice, AI
Overviews. Mostly on-site.

**AIO — AI Overviews.** Google's AI-generated answer summaries. A platform
feature, not a strategy. Anyone selling "AIO services" is describing a feature as
a discipline.

**Answer engine.** A system that responds to a question with a synthesised answer
rather than a list of links.

**Answer-first writing.** Placing a direct, self-contained 40–60 word answer
immediately after a heading, before any elaboration. The highest-leverage on-site
change for AEO.

**Baseline.** The measured starting position before any work. Without it, no
result can be proven.

**Citation share.** Percentage of prompt runs in which the client is cited with a
link. Stricter and more valuable than mention rate.

**Entity consistency.** Identical business name, category, and description across
every property on the web. Helps engines resolve you as one entity.

**Extraction.** An engine lifting text from a page more or less verbatim. The AEO
mechanism.

**GEO — Generative Engine Optimization.** Building the authority and presence that
cause an LLM to cite you when synthesising an answer. Mostly off-site.

**LLMO.** Another term used roughly interchangeably with GEO. The terminology in
this space is not settled.

**llms.txt.** A root-level plain-text file summarising a site for LLMs. Low cost,
unproven impact. Do not oversell.

**Maskable icon.** An app icon with content inside a safe zone so Android can crop
it to any shape without cutting the mark.

**Mention rate.** Percentage of prompt runs in which the client is named, cited or
not.

**Non-determinism.** The property that the same prompt can produce different
answers on consecutive runs. The reason guarantees are impossible and three runs
per prompt are mandatory.

**Prompt set.** The fixed, versioned collection of 40–50 prompts used to measure
visibility. The core instrument of the business.

**RDAP.** Registration Data Access Protocol — the official successor to WHOIS.
Used by the domain-finder tool, not part of client work.

**Retrieval layer.** The live web search an engine performs at query time.
Reachable, fast-moving, and the target of all our work.

**Structured data / JSON-LD / schema.** Machine-readable markup describing page
content. Helps both extraction and entity resolution.

**Synthesis.** An engine blending multiple sources into one answer. The GEO
mechanism.

**Training layer.** A model's baseline knowledge, fixed until retraining. Not
reachable. Never promise anything about it.
